echr-py

Open-source toolkit for acquiring and analyzing case law from the European Court of Human Rights

The European Court of Human Rights publishes over 20,000 decisions through HUDOC, a public database maintained by the Council of Europe. Every judgment, admissibility decision, and opinion sits there in multiple languages, preserved and searchable. The structure is there—sections, paragraphs, citations—but accessing it requires infrastructure. You need the exact wording of a paragraph cited in a separate opinion. You need to know which cases cite which others, and whether the subsequent case distinguished or followed. You need this not as a human reader browsing case-by-case, but as a corpus, reproducibly, with enough metadata to trace the provenance of every piece.

This is where echr-py enters. It is a research infrastructure toolkit written in Python. Point it at HUDOC. Give it search parameters—articles, respondents, dates. The toolkit fetches what exists, parses the structure, resolves citations to their exact targets, and exports the result in formats designed for research: parquet for statistical analysis, JSON-LD for graph work, HTML for interactive reading. Every fragment of data carries source metadata. A paragraph can be traced back to its document, language version, checksum, and the software version that extracted it. This is not for convenience. This is for research that needs to survive scrutiny.

The data below examines 1,200 cases drawn from HUDOC in 2025 and 2026. The picture is not complete—the database contains older decisions, parallel language versions, separate opinions, and procedural variations—but it reflects what a researcher starting now would encounter if they queried the system and asked: what is the shape of the work you do.

Citation network: how cases connect

Each node is a case. Lines show citations: one case refers to another in its reasoning. The clusters reveal doctrinal communities where cases cite each other densely. Larger nodes appear more frequently in others' citations. The network shows how precedent flows through the Court's work—some cases sit at the periphery, rarely cited; others occupy the center, woven into the fabric of ongoing jurisprudence.

Article citations: what the Court reasons about

Article 6—right to a fair trial—appears in 494 of 1,200 cases, 41%. Article 6-1 specifies the public hearing requirement. Together they represent two-thirds of the Court's procedural work. Article 35 on admissibility comes next. This reflects the legal structure: the Court must first determine whether a case can proceed before ruling on merits.

Respondent states: who is sued

Ukraine and Russia together account for 255 cases, 21% of the sample. Italy, Turkey, and Romania follow. This reflects both complaint volume and admissibility rates. Some states have high complaint-to-adjudication ratios; others reject many complaints at admissibility. The data measures caseload distribution, not compliance.

How the toolkit works

The pipeline moves through stages. You query HUDOC with parameters—articles, respondents, dates. The toolkit fetches metadata first. For each result, it downloads the full text and parses it. It resolves citations: when the Court says "see paragraph 42 of Smith v. UK," it finds that paragraph. It builds a graph where nodes are paragraphs and edges are citations. You export in whatever format serves next—an HTML viewer for close reading, a parquet file for statistical analysis, a JSON-LD graph for linking to other data.

The work is deterministic. Run it twice on the same query and you get identical results. Every artifact is source-addressed: this paragraph came from this HUDOC document, version this checksum, processed by this code revision. The provenance is first-class data, not an afterthought. This matters for research where replicability matters.

The toolkit is open source. MIT license. You install it in Python. You run it locally. You own your data when it completes. No API rate limits. No vendor lock-in.

Why this matters

Human rights law is citation-intensive. Every argument rests on precedent. The European Court issues thousands of decisions. Tracking which cases control which later outcomes, identifying the line of reasoning, building datasets that test whether a case actually determined future behavior—this is hard by hand. It requires seeing the whole network.

echr-py is one answer. It doesn't ask you to trust a vendor's summarization. It doesn't lock you into a platform's search syntax. It gives you the raw material: structured, provenance-intact, in formats you control. You build your own analysis on top.

The research questions that follow are open. Which articles have accelerated in citation frequency? Do some countries receive more expansive interpretations of the Convention? When the Court cites its own earlier decisions, how does the reasoning evolve? Does reversing or distinguishing a precedent happen in bursts, tied to a change in the Court's composition? These questions need data. They need infrastructure. They need a tool.
