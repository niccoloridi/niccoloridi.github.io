echr-py

Niccolò Ridi
A Python toolkit for empirical research on the European Court of Human Rights (2026)
524 Grand Chamber judgments · 4,416 citations · 1994–2026
github · pypi

The European Court of Human Rights has published more than thirty thousand decisions since 1959. Each one cites earlier ones. Together those references form a network – a map of which decisions the Court keeps returning to, and which it decided once and left behind. echr-py is a Python toolkit that turns HUDOC, the Court's public database, into research-ready datasets: search and download, citation extraction, network export.

This page does three things. It describes the data and the problems it poses. It shows the toolkit's core commands, each one copyable. And it uses both to replicate the central finding of Voeten and Lupu's 2011 study of precedent in international courts – on every Grand Chamber judgment the Court has issued, 1994 to 2026.

I. The Data and the Question

HUDOC publishes every judgment, decision, and order the Court issues. Each record is structured: application number, case name, respondent state, the Convention articles at issue, the judgment date, an importance rating from 1 to 4, whether any judge wrote separately, and whether a Committee, a Chamber, or the Grand Chamber decided it. That structure is what makes automated analysis possible at all.

The question Voeten and Lupu asked is simple to state and hard to answer by reading. Not all citations carry the same weight. A decision cited fifty times shapes European law more than one cited twice. So which decisions anchor doctrine, and why those?

Their answer was strategic. International courts operate without coercive enforcement, so they build legitimacy by embedding each new decision in a visible chain of past reasoning. Citation is not just reference; it is justification. Which means the shape of the citation network tells you something about how the Court manages its own authority.

The problems

Language. Judgments appear in several official languages, and the same case is cited differently across them. Resolution. Not every reference resolves cleanly to a target document; some name a case without an application number. Recency. The Court's output grows every year, so any date-bounded corpus over-represents recent decisions and under-represents the older ones that have had time to accumulate citations. Selection. HUDOC holds published decisions. What was never published is invisible, and invisible in a way that is not random.

Welp — I double counted

An earlier draft of this essay said there were 1,048 Grand Chamber judgments. There are 524. The mistake is worth leaving on the page, because it is the easiest one to make with this data and because nothing about it looks like a mistake.

search() and count default to languages=("ENG", "FRE"). Almost every Grand Chamber judgment is published in both English and French, and HUDOC stores each language version as its own document, with its own item ID. Ask for both and every judgment comes back twice. The number that lands is plausible, and it sorts and filters like any other number.

One line each way settles it:

$echr-py count --body grand-chamber --collection JUDGMENTS
1048
$echr-py count --body grand-chamber --collection JUDGMENTS --language ENG
524
$echr-py count --body grand-chamber --collection JUDGMENTS --language FRE
524

524 and 524. Exactly double, which is the signature of the problem: a language artefact scales by a clean factor, where a real finding rarely does. The same trap runs through the whole collection. Its 58,427 “judgments” are 29,213 English and 29,214 French – and that stray extra one is a judgment with a French text and no English sibling, which is its own small lesson about what “the corpus” is.

So: pin the language. Every figure below counts each judgment once.

None of these are fatal. All of them shape what the numbers below can and cannot support.

II. The Core Functions

Three commands cover most of the pipeline. Each line below is individually copyable.

1. Search and download

The search() function queries HUDOC by article, respondent state, date range, and importance level, and returns a typed collection with the metadata attached.

$pip install "echr-py[citations,analysis]"
Successfully installed echr-py-0.2.0
>>>from hudoc_py import search
>>>cases = search(article="6", respondent="TUR", year_from=2015)
Fetching metadata... [████████████] 87/87 Downloaded 87 cases (72 with full text)
>>>print(cases[0].name, cases[0].articles, cases[0].kp_date)

2. Citation extraction

The corpus build pipeline parses the judgment text, finds citations in the body, the footnotes, and any separate opinions, and resolves each one to a target document with a paragraph pinpoint where the text supplies it.

$echr-py corpus build --appno 46221/99 \
--rich-sections --citations --out ocalan/
Parsing document... ✓ 2,145 blocks parsed Extracting citations... ✓ 127 citations resolved (94 internal) Building graph... ✓ 58 unique targets verified Written to ocalan/cases.parquet
>>>import pandas as pd
>>>df = pd.read_parquet('ocalan/cases.parquet')
>>>print(df[['name', 'respondent', 'articles', 'dissent']].head())

3. Network export

Export the citation graph as JSON, GraphML, or a NetworkX object. Each edge is a citation: case A cites case B.

$echr-py graph export --kind case-case \
--format json --in corpus/citations --out network.json
✓ Exported network with 525 nodes and 4416 edges
>>>import json, networkx as nx
>>>G = nx.node_link_graph(json.load(open('network.json')))
>>>print(G.number_of_nodes(), G.number_of_edges(), round(nx.density(G), 4))

III. Replicating Voeten & Lupu

The replication measures in-degree centrality: how many other judgments cite a given one. High in-degree marks a hub – a judgment the Court keeps returning to when it needs to ground a new holding.

Which judgments go into the corpus turns out to matter more than the measure does. Here are two corpora of exactly the same size, put through exactly the same pipeline.

First corpus: a thin recent slice

Take the Court's most recent 524 judgments – 16 December 2025 to 23 July 2026, seven months of output across every bench. Their SCL fields carry 4,063 citations between them. Resolve those against the corpus itself and almost nothing lands.

524Judgments
4,063Citations made
6Edges inside corpus
1%Ever cited

Six edges out of four thousand references. The network is not sparse so much as absent. The median judgment cites nothing inside the corpus and is cited by nothing inside it.

It would be easy to write that up as a finding about the Court: a long tail, a few hubs, precedent thinly spread. It is not a finding about the Court. It is a finding about the window. Judgments cite earlier judgments, and in a seven-month frame almost every earlier judgment sits outside the frame. The 4,057 references that failed to resolve did not fail – they pointed out of the box. Cut a citation network anywhere in time and this is what falls out.

Second corpus: the complete Grand Chamber

Now take a corpus that is closed rather than clipped. Every Grand Chamber judgment the Court has handed down, 1994 to 2026. There are 524 of them – the same size as the slice above, which is a coincidence, and a convenient one.

525Nodes
4,416Edges
8.41Mean in-degree
1.61%Density

Same method, same number of judgments, and the network is roughly seven hundred times denser: 1.61% against 0.0022%. 86% of Grand Chamber judgments are cited by at least one other Grand Chamber judgment. The median is cited six times. The most-cited is cited sixty-two times.

The reason is structural rather than doctrinal. The Grand Chamber is where the Court sends what it means to settle, and it is the bench that cites itself. The population is closed, so no citation falls off the edge of a window. It is small enough that its members genuinely refer to one another, and old enough – thirty-two years – that the early judgments have had time to be picked up. This is the Court's own canon, and it behaves like one.

The most interesting split is the one the articles alone would not give you. Article 6 does not form a single cluster: it breaks into a civil limb (Roche, Z and Others – access to court, immunities) and a criminal limb (Gäfgen, Sejdović – evidence, trial in absentia), each citing internally and rarely across. The Court's own citation practice treats them as two bodies of law that happen to share a provision number. Nothing in the metadata says that. The edges do.

K. and T. v. Finland sits at the top with sixty-two citations: the reference point for the Court's Article 8 case law on taking children into care. Kudła created the effective-remedy requirement for excessive length of proceedings, and is cited by nearly everything that followed it. Broniowski was the first pilot judgment. McCann, from 1995, is still where Article 2 and lethal force begins. Age helps but does not decide: Radomilja is from 2018 and already has thirty-two citations, because it settled what the “scope of the case” means.

What the Grand Chamber is made of

The composition looks nothing like the Court's output as a whole, which is the point of having the bench at all.

Importance. HUDOC rates each judgment from 1 (case-law significance) down to 4. The Grand Chamber splits 387 / 112 / 14 / 11 – 74% at Level 1. Across the Court as a whole that ratio is inverted: most of what Strasbourg does is apply settled law to new facts. The Grand Chamber is the designed exception.

Separate opinions. 430 of the 524 judgments carry one – 82%. Disagreement is the norm at this bench rather than the exception, which follows from what gets referred to it: the questions that divide.

Figure 2: Share of Grand Chamber judgments carrying a separate opinion, by Convention article.

The spread by article is narrow, and the ordering is not the obvious one. Article 14, discrimination, draws separate opinions in 90% of the Grand Chamber cases where it is in issue – the highest of any article. Article 6, fair trial, draws them in 79%, the lowest. Discrimination turns on contested judgments about comparators and justification. Fair-trial questions at this level are more often technical, and the bench agrees about procedure more readily than it agrees about equality.

Temporal distribution

Grand Chamber judgments run from 1994 to 2026, and the shape is not the rising curve the Court's overall docket shows. There is a spike in 1999 – 63 judgments, when Protocol 11 stood up the permanent Court and a backlog of Commission cases came through – then a fluctuating band of roughly ten to thirty a year, thinning to single figures since 2024. Referral is rare by design, and the bench has not grown with the caseload.

Respondent states

The United Kingdom leads with 68 Grand Chamber judgments, then Türkiye (58), Italy (49), and France (46). That ordering differs from the Court's overall docket, where Türkiye and Russia dominate by sheer volume. Referral to the Grand Chamber tracks legal novelty, not case count: a state can generate thousands of repetitive applications and very few referrals.

IV. All the interesting things you can do with this

Temporal trajectories of doctrine. Does Article 6 jurisprudence follow a line, or are there reversals? When does a case go from obscure to hub? Track a single decision's centrality year by year and you get a curve rather than a number.

Citation sentiment. A citation can build on a case, distinguish it into irrelevance, or overrule it outright. The bare edge does not say which. Parse the surrounding sentence and the network acquires a sign.

Judge influence. Which judges write the decisions that later get cited? Do particular drafting styles or reasoning structures propagate?

Network evolution. Does the graph densify over time, or thin out? Are there structural breaks – moments where the citation pattern reorganises around a new anchor?

Article co-occurrence. Which articles are pleaded together? Does Article 6 always travel with Article 13? The unexpected pairings are where the interesting tensions live.

Dissent as a leading indicator. Do dissents in hub cases become majority doctrine later? If so, dissent patterns forecast where the law is about to move.

State-level networks. Build a separate graph per respondent. Do Turkish and German cases cite different precedents for the same article?

The toolkit supplies the pipeline. HUDOC supplies the documents. The questions are yours.

Coda

524 judgments and 4,416 citations show a Court reasoning in public and building on itself as it goes. The hubs are not accidents. They are the decisions that survived being tested against new facts and kept their footing, and the Court returns to them because returning to them is how it makes a new holding look inevitable rather than invented.

Voeten and Lupu argued that citation networks reveal a legitimation strategy. The Grand Chamber corpus is consistent with that: a small canon carries most of the weight, foundational judgments hold their position across decades, and 82% of the bench's output carries a separate opinion – the disagreement is on the record, not hidden. The network is not random. It is the Court reasoning itself into authority.
