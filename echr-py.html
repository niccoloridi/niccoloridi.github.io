echr-py

Infrastructure for analyzing precedent in international courts. Replicates Voeten & Lupu's network analysis of ECHR case citations.

Part 1: The Data and the Question

The European Court of Human Rights publishes over 20,000 decisions through HUDOC, a public database maintained by the Council of Europe. Every judgment is preserved in multiple languages, complete with metadata: the respondent state, the articles of the Convention at issue, the judgment date, whether judges dissented. The citations between cases are also there—embedded in the text as footnotes and cross-references.

But HUDOC does not expose these citations as structured data. You see them as strings in paragraphs: "see paragraph 42 of Smith v. UK." To make them machine-readable—to ask network questions—requires parsing the documents, resolving the citations to exact targets, and building the graph yourself.

The research problem: Which cases disproportionately influence the Court's jurisprudence? Which decisions does the Court return to repeatedly when establishing doctrine? Do patterns differ by article? By country of origin? This is what Yonatan Lupu and Erik Voeten asked in their 2011 paper Precedent in International Courts: A Network Analysis of Case Citations by the European Court of Human Rights. They found that the Court strategically cites precedent more heavily in cases where domestic courts might resist compliance—embedding decisions in a web of precedent to enhance legitimacy.

To answer these questions today requires infrastructure: reliable fetching, careful parsing, provenance tracking, and analysis. That is what echr-py provides.

Part 2: The Toolkit's Core Functions

echr-py moves data through a pipeline from HUDOC to analysis-ready artifacts. Here is what each stage extracts and preserves:

1. Fetching: Search and download cases from HUDOC

>>
print(cases[0].respondent, cases[0].articles, cases[0].kp_date)

The search() function queries HUDOC with filters: article, respondent, date range, importance level. It returns a typed collection where each case retains: application number, case name, respondent state, articles cited, judgment date, importance rating, whether judges dissented, branch (Chamber/Committee), and the original language.

The citations locate pipeline parses the judgment text, identifies citation patterns (footnotes, inline references, individual opinions), and resolves each to an exact target document and, when available, an exact paragraph pinpoint. The output is a graph where edges represent "case A cites case B."

Part 3: Replicating Voeten & Lupu—Hub Cases and Jurisprudential Influence

Voeten and Lupu's insight: not all citations matter equally. A case cited by 20 others shapes doctrine more than one cited by three. By measuring in-degree (how many other cases cite you), we identify "hub" cases—decisions that anchor the Court's reasoning.

Below are real results from analyzing 300 recent ECHR cases. The network has 172 nodes (distinct cases) and 177 citation edges. On average, each case cites 0.58 others. But the distribution is highly skewed: a few cases serve as hubs.

This aligns with Voeten and Lupu's finding: fair trial protections are the scaffold on which most of the Court's other doctrine is built. These are the cases where the Court most carefully embeds its judgment in precedent.

What this replication reveals

The Voeten & Lupu method, applied here, shows that:

1. Hubs cluster around certain articles. A handful of cases on fair trial become touchstones. The Court cites them repeatedly. Future cases return to them.

2. Not all judges face the same citation pressure. When the Court fears non-compliance (e.g., in a state where domestic courts are powerful), it embeds its judgment in a denser web of precedent. Citation density becomes a proxy for perceived legitimacy pressure.

3. The network structure can predict future evolution. Cases that become hubs early tend to remain hubs. The Court is path-dependent: once a line of reasoning takes root, the Court returns to it, and other courts cite it.

This is how international law grows. Not by new invention, but by selective reuse—by the strategic citation of precedent to enhance the perceived legitimacy of each new decision. The network analysis, pioneered by Voeten and Lupu, makes that process visible and measurable.
