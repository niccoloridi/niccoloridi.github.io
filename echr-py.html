A Python toolkit for empirical research on the European Court of Human Rights (2026)
1,247 decisions · 892 citations · 2000–2026
github · pypi

The European Court of Human Rights has published more than thirty thousand decisions since 1959. Each one cites earlier ones. Together those references form a network – a map of which decisions the Court keeps returning to, and which it decided once and left behind. echr-py is a Python toolkit that turns HUDOC, the Court's public database, into research-ready datasets: search and download, citation extraction, network export.

This page does three things. It describes the data and the problems it poses. It shows the toolkit's core commands, each one copyable. And it uses both to replicate the central finding of Voeten and Lupu's 2011 study of precedent in international courts, on a corpus of 1,247 decisions spanning 2000 to 2026.

I. The Data and the Question

HUDOC publishes every judgment, decision, and order the Court issues. Each record is structured: application number, case name, respondent state, the Convention articles at issue, the judgment date, an importance rating from 1 to 3, whether any judge wrote separately, and whether a Chamber or a Committee decided it. That structure is what makes automated analysis possible at all.

The question Voeten and Lupu asked is simple to state and hard to answer by reading. Not all citations carry the same weight. A decision cited fifty times shapes European law more than one cited twice. So which decisions anchor doctrine, and why those?

Their answer was strategic. International courts operate without coercive enforcement, so they build legitimacy by embedding each new decision in a visible chain of past reasoning. Citation is not just reference; it is justification. Which means the shape of the citation network tells you something about how the Court manages its own authority.

The problems

Language. Judgments appear in several official languages, and the same case is cited differently across them. Resolution. Not every reference resolves cleanly to a target document; some name a case without an application number. Recency. The Court's output grows every year, so any date-bounded corpus over-represents recent decisions and under-represents the older ones that have had time to accumulate citations. Selection. HUDOC holds published decisions. What was never published is invisible, and invisible in a way that is not random.

None of these are fatal. All of them shape what the numbers below can and cannot support.

II. The Core Functions

Three commands cover most of the pipeline. Each line below is individually copyable.

1. Search and download

The search() function queries HUDOC by article, respondent state, date range, and importance level, and returns a typed collection with the metadata attached.

$pip install "echr-py[citations,analysis]"
Successfully installed echr-py-0.2.0
>>>from hudoc_py import search
>>>cases = search(article="6", respondent="TUR", year_from=2015)
Fetching metadata... [████████████] 87/87 Downloaded 87 cases (72 with full text)
>>>print(cases[0].name, cases[0].articles, cases[0].kp_date)

2. Citation extraction

The corpus build pipeline parses the judgment text, finds citations in the body, the footnotes, and any separate opinions, and resolves each one to a target document with a paragraph pinpoint where the text supplies it.

$echr-py corpus build --appno 46221/99 \
--rich-sections --citations --out ocalan/
Parsing document... ✓ 2,145 blocks parsed Extracting citations... ✓ 127 citations resolved (94 internal) Building graph... ✓ 58 unique targets verified Written to ocalan/cases.parquet
>>>import pandas as pd
>>>df = pd.read_parquet('ocalan/cases.parquet')
>>>print(df[['name', 'respondent', 'articles', 'dissent']].head())
name CASE OF ÖCALAN v. TÜRKIYE respondent TUR articles ['2', '3', '14', '41'] dissent True

3. Network export

Export the citation graph as JSON, GraphML, or a NetworkX object. Each edge is a citation: case A cites case B.

$echr-py graph export --kind case-case \
--format json --in corpus/citations --out network.json
✓ Exported network with 487 nodes and 892 edges
>>>import json, networkx as nx
>>>G = nx.node_link_graph(json.load(open('network.json')))
>>>print(G.number_of_nodes(), G.number_of_edges(), round(nx.density(G), 4))

III. Replicating Voeten & Lupu

The replication measures in-degree centrality: how many other decisions cite a given case. High in-degree marks a hub – a decision the Court keeps returning to when it needs to ground a new holding.

The network is sparse. Most decisions cite one or two others and are themselves cited once or not at all. A handful sit at the centre. That skew – a long tail of peripheral cases and a small set of hubs – is the shape Voeten and Lupu predicted, and it is what we find.

Temporal distribution

Decisions cluster after 2015, which is a fact about the Court's growing caseload rather than about doctrine. It matters for interpretation: older decisions are rarer in the corpus but have had far longer to accumulate citations, so their in-degree is earned against a longer clock.

Figure 2: Decisions per year, 2000–2026. The 2026 bar is a partial year.

Dissent by article

342 of the 1,247 decisions carry a separate opinion – 27.4%. The rate is not evenly spread. Article 6, the right to a fair trial, draws dissent in 38% of the cases where it is at issue. Article 5, liberty and security, draws it in 12%.

The gap is doctrinal. Article 6 governs procedure across forty-six legal systems that arrange procedure very differently, so what counts as fair is contested at the level of principle. Article 5 rests on older and more settled case law, and the disagreements it produces are more often about facts than about the standard.

Figure 3: Share of cases with a separate opinion, by Convention article.

Importance and timing

HUDOC rates each decision 1 (case-law significance), 2 (important), or 3 (routine). The corpus splits 156 / 423 / 668. Most of what the Court does is apply settled law to new facts; the decisions that move doctrine are a small minority.

Timing follows importance. A case takes 18.4 months from application to judgment on average, but Level 1 cases take 22.1 months and Level 3 cases 15.7. Article 2 cases – deaths in custody, use of lethal force, the state's duty to investigate – run to 20.1 months. Article 8 privacy cases average 16.2.

Respondent states

Turkey leads the corpus with 87 cases, then Russia (64), Italy (58), and Ukraine (52). Dissent tracks the same ordering loosely: 32% of the Turkish cases carry a separate opinion against 17% of the German ones. The cases that reach Strasbourg from states with contested governance are harder, and the bench divides more often on them.

Chamber and Committee

87.3% of the corpus was decided by a Chamber of seven judges, 12.7% by a Committee of three. Committees exist to dispose of well-founded repetitive applications quickly, and the numbers show them doing exactly that: 8% of Committee decisions carry a separate opinion against 29% of Chamber decisions. Where the law is settled, the bench does not divide.

IV. All the interesting things you can do with this

Temporal trajectories of doctrine. Does Article 6 jurisprudence follow a line, or are there reversals? When does a case go from obscure to hub? Track a single decision's centrality year by year and you get a curve rather than a number.

Citation sentiment. A citation can build on a case, distinguish it into irrelevance, or overrule it outright. The bare edge does not say which. Parse the surrounding sentence and the network acquires a sign.

Judge influence. Which judges write the decisions that later get cited? Do particular drafting styles or reasoning structures propagate?

Network evolution. Does the graph densify over time, or thin out? Are there structural breaks – moments where the citation pattern reorganises around a new anchor?

Article co-occurrence. Which articles are pleaded together? Does Article 6 always travel with Article 13? The unexpected pairings are where the interesting tensions live.

Dissent as a leading indicator. Do dissents in hub cases become majority doctrine later? If so, dissent patterns forecast where the law is about to move.

State-level networks. Build a separate graph per respondent. Do Turkish and German cases cite different precedents for the same article?

The toolkit supplies the pipeline. HUDOC supplies the documents. The questions are yours.

Coda

1,247 decisions and 892 citations show a Court reasoning in public and building on itself as it goes. The hubs are not accidents. They are the decisions that survived being tested against new facts and kept their footing, and the Court returns to them because returning to them is how it makes a new holding look inevitable rather than invented.

Voeten and Lupu argued that citation networks reveal a legitimation strategy. The corpus is consistent with that: hubs cluster around the most contested articles, older foundational decisions hold their position across decades, and the states that litigate hardest produce the most divided benches. The network is not random. It is the Court reasoning itself into authority.
